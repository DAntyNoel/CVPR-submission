\begin{abstract}
Preference tuning can improve the short answers of vision-language models
(VLMs), but a correct yes/no answer does not necessarily imply that the model
has grounded the answer in the right visual evidence. We introduce
\benchmark{}, a compact diagnostic benchmark for claim-evidence consistency.
The benchmark is built from \dataset{}, a 6k-row collection of object,
attribute, relation, and wrong-evidence preference examples derived from COCO
and GQA annotations with zero train/eval image overlap against the fixed
evaluation suite. Unlike ordinary answer-level evaluation, \benchmark{} asks a
model to judge whether a candidate visual claim is supported by a specific
evidence label or relation, including same-answer cases where only the evidence
is wrong. We evaluate a base VLM, an answer-only DPO baseline, and one
evidence-aware preference-tuning baseline on both ordinary short-answer
benchmarks and \benchmark{} probes. The resulting diagnosis is that
short-answer gains and evidence consistency are separable: a model can improve
ordinary yes/no accuracy while remaining weak at rejecting mismatched evidence,
especially for spatial relations. These findings suggest that future grounded
preference-tuning work should report evidence consistency directly rather than
using answer accuracy alone as a proxy for grounding.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Vision-language models can answer simple visual questions fluently while using
the wrong visual support. For example, a model may answer that a bus is present
because the image contains a large vehicle, or claim that a cup is left of a
plate while relying on the reversed relation. In such cases, answer correctness
and evidence correctness are related but not identical. This distinction is
important for preference tuning, where training pairs often specify which
answer is preferred without checking whether the preferred answer is tied to
the right visual evidence.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is attractive for VLM alignment because it
uses paired preferences without training a separate reward model. However, an
answer-only preference pair can be satisfied by learning a better response
prior or yes/no bias. Even evidence-augmented response templates may fail to
test the intended behavior if evaluation only asks for a short answer. We
therefore ask a diagnostic question: when preference tuning changes the final
answer, does it also improve the model's ability to verify claim-evidence
consistency?

We introduce \benchmark{}, a small benchmark designed to isolate this question.
The benchmark uses object, attribute, and left/right relation claims with
annotation-derived evidence. Its key slice is wrong-evidence verification:
examples where the answer can remain plausible, but the candidate evidence is
mismatched. This forces evaluation to look beyond answer text and ask whether
the model accepts the correct evidence object, attribute, or relation.

Our goal is not to claim that a new preference-tuning method solves visual
hallucination. Instead, we use \benchmark{} as a diagnostic instrument. We
compare a base model, an answer-only DPO model, and an evidence-aware
preference-tuned model under the same backbone, data scale, and evaluation
suite. We report both ordinary short-answer transfer and explicit
claim-evidence metrics.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We define \benchmark{}, a compact claim-evidence consistency
    benchmark that separates answer correctness from evidence correctness.
    \item We construct \dataset{}, a 6k-row controlled set with object,
    attribute, relation, and wrong-evidence examples, plus fixed supported and
    wrong-evidence probe splits.
    \item We provide a diagnostic evaluation protocol for VLM preference
    tuning that reports short-answer transfer, evidence-label matching,
    relation-direction accuracy, wrong-evidence rejection, and invalid-format
    rates.
    \item We show, through controlled baselines, that answer-level preference
    gains do not automatically imply stronger evidence verification.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Object hallucination and VLM evaluation.}
Prior work studies hallucination by checking generated captions or answers
against objects and relations present in the image. Polling-style yes/no
benchmarks are useful because they make unsupported object claims measurable.
\benchmark{} follows this controlled evaluation spirit, but asks whether the
model can connect a claim to the evidence that supports it.

\paragraph{Preference tuning.}
DPO and related preference-optimization methods provide a practical route for
aligning models from chosen/rejected examples. In VLM settings, answer-level
preferences can improve final responses while leaving the grounding signal
implicit. Our benchmark is designed to test whether such tuning changes
evidence verification, not only answer selection.

\paragraph{Grounding and rationales.}
Grounded VLM training can use boxes, masks, region descriptions, or human
rationales. These signals are expensive and often evaluated through task
accuracy. \benchmark{} uses weaker but cheap annotation-derived evidence labels
and relations, allowing a small diagnostic study that can be regenerated from
existing COCO/GQA-style resources.

\section{\benchmark{} Benchmark}

\subsection{Task Definition}

Each \benchmark{} example contains an image, a question, a visual claim, and a
candidate evidence item. The model must return a structured judgment indicating
whether the evidence supports the claim. The expected output contains four
fields:
\begin{equation}
    \{\texttt{answer}, \texttt{claim}, \texttt{evidence\_label}, \texttt{support}\}.
\end{equation}
The support label is one of \texttt{supported}, \texttt{contradicted}, or
\texttt{wrong\_evidence}.

The wrong-evidence slice is central. It includes examples where the answer text
alone is not enough to distinguish chosen from rejected behavior. For instance,
both responses may concern the same visible object, but the rejected evidence
points to a different object label or an incorrect relation.

\subsection{Schema}

The canonical row stores the image path and stable image id, question,
chosen/rejected answers, chosen/rejected claim blocks, task type, negative
type, and evidence metadata. Boxes are retained when available for audit and
future region-aware extensions, but the primary probe scorer uses text labels,
attributes, and relation direction.

\subsection{Leakage and Audit Checks}

The current split has zero train/eval image overlap against the fixed internal
and external evaluation suite. Before submission, we additionally require a
manual audit of 100--200 sampled examples, covering answer correctness,
evidence correctness, relation direction, and wrong-evidence construction.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}

\subsection{Model Groups}

The main comparison uses at most three groups:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Base Instruct:} the untuned VLM.
    \item \textbf{\answerdpo{}:} an answer-only DPO baseline trained on the
    same canonical questions and answer preferences.
    \item \textbf{\evidencebaseline{}:} the selected evidence-aware baseline.
    This is CEPO-Dual if final results pass the short-answer and probe gates;
    otherwise it is CEPO-Latent and is presented as a failed latent-transfer
    baseline.
\end{itemize}

All trained groups use the same backbone, LoRA-DPO recipe, training image pool,
and one-epoch small-data setting. Heavy training and inference are run through
Slurm.

\subsection{Short-Answer Transfer}

We first evaluate ordinary yes/no behavior on COCO object existence, GQA
simple, Hard COCO, and Base-error-mined diagnostics, with POPE and AMBER as
external sanity checks when complete. Metrics are accuracy, balanced accuracy,
F1, false-positive rate, false-negative rate, yes rate, and invalid/other rate.

\subsection{\benchmark{} Probes}

We then evaluate supported-evidence and wrong-evidence probes. Metrics are:
support accuracy, evidence-label accuracy, claim-match rate,
relation-direction accuracy, wrong-evidence rejection accuracy, and invalid
JSON rate. The headline metric is wrong-evidence rejection because it tests the
case where plausible answer text is not enough.

\section{Discussion}

\benchmark{} is designed to expose a failure mode that is hidden by ordinary
answer accuracy. Answer-only preference tuning can improve the final yes/no
response by changing recall or yes bias. That behavior is useful, but it does
not prove that the model checks whether a claim is supported by the correct
object, attribute, or relation. Wrong-evidence probes make this distinction
visible.

The benchmark also clarifies how evidence-aware training should be evaluated.
If a model is trained to generate claim blocks but tested only with short
answers, improvements may not transfer. Conversely, if a model improves
structured evidence probes while losing short-answer accuracy, it is better
understood as an evidence verifier than as a general answerer. Reporting both
views prevents overclaiming.

\section{Limitations}

\benchmark{} is intentionally small and controlled. It is derived from COCO and
GQA annotations rather than newly collected human evidence labels. The current
evidence representation is mostly textual: object labels, attributes, and
left/right relations, with boxes retained for audit where available. The
rule-based scorer is transparent and cheap, but it may mark some semantically
valid free-form outputs as mismatches. Finally, the current experiments use a
single backbone and small LoRA-DPO runs; the benchmark should be extended to
more model families before drawing broad conclusions about VLM alignment.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented \benchmark{}, a diagnostic benchmark for claim-evidence
consistency in VLM preference tuning. The benchmark separates ordinary
short-answer performance from the ability to verify whether a visual claim is
supported by the correct evidence. The resulting paper framing is deliberately
diagnostic: evidence consistency should be measured directly, because
short-answer gains alone can hide persistent wrong-evidence failures. This
compact benchmark provides a practical testbed for future grounded preference
tuning methods while avoiding the unsupported claim that lightweight evidence
formatting alone solves visual hallucination.
