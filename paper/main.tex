\begin{abstract}
Preference tuning can improve the short answers of vision-language models, but
a correct answer does not guarantee that the model has verified the right
visual evidence. We introduce \emph{Claim-Evidence Preference Optimization}
Probe (\benchmark{}), a controlled diagnostic benchmark for claim-evidence
consistency, built from \dataset{}, a 6k-row annotation-derived collection of
object, attribute, relation, and wrong-evidence examples from COCO and GQA.
Unlike ordinary yes/no evaluation, the benchmark asks whether a candidate
visual claim is supported by a specific evidence label or relation, including
same-answer cases where only the evidence is wrong. Under a fixed
Qwen2.5-VL-7B backbone, we compare a base model, an answer-only tuned model, a
verifier-only control, and two mixed answer/verifier settings. The results show
that answer preference and evidence verification can move in opposite
directions: answer-only tuning improves short-answer accuracy but lowers
wrong-evidence rejection from 44.3\% to 34.3\%. Direct verifier supervision
recovers this behavior, reaching 53.0\% in the verifier-only control and
70.3\% in the strongest mixed setting while preserving the answer-level gains.
Row-bootstrap intervals, parser audits, seed stability, and slice analysis
support a bounded conclusion: explicit verifier preferences substantially
improve object and attribute evidence checks, but subject/object role swaps
remain fragile. External POPE and AMBER checks stay close to the answer-only
baseline, so the claim is diagnostic rather than a broad
hallucination-benchmark win.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Vision-language models can answer visual questions fluently while accepting
the wrong visual support. A model may correctly answer that a sink is present
but accept ``potted plant'' as the evidence, or it may accept a left/right
relation after the subject and object have been swapped. These errors matter
because many preference-tuning pipelines supervise the final answer, while the
connection between the answer and its visual evidence remains implicit.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)~\cite{dpo} is attractive for VLM
alignment because it trains from chosen/rejected pairs without learning a
separate reward model. However, an answer-only preference pair can be satisfied
by learning a better response prior or a stronger yes/no bias. If evaluation
also asks only for short answers, a model can appear improved while still
accepting evidence that contradicts the intended visual claim. This raises a
diagnostic question: when preference tuning improves ordinary answers, does it
also improve the ability to verify claim-evidence consistency?

We introduce \emph{Claim-Evidence Preference Optimization Probe}
(\benchmark{}), a controlled diagnostic benchmark designed to isolate this
question. Each example contains an image, a question, a candidate claim, and a
candidate evidence item. The model returns a structured judgment indicating
whether the evidence supports the claim. The key split is wrong-evidence
verification: the answer text may remain plausible, but the candidate evidence
is mismatched. This forces evaluation to look beyond the final answer and ask
whether the model accepts the correct object, attribute, or spatial relation.

We focus on a controlled diagnostic setting. \dataset{} contains 6{,}000
training rows derived from existing COCO~\cite{coco} and GQA~\cite{gqa}
annotations, with a fixed 600-row supported-evidence probe and 400-row
wrong-evidence probe. The main paper limits the training comparison to five
groups: Base Instruct, \answerdpo{}, \evidencedpo{}, \dualonek{}, and
\dualtwok{}. This keeps the experimental surface narrow enough for a
reproducible diagnostic conclusion while still testing whether verifier
supervision helps by itself and when mixed with answer preferences.

Our conclusion is deliberately bounded. We do not claim that \dualtwok{}
solves visual hallucination. Instead, \benchmark{} shows that answer-level DPO
and evidence verification can move independently. \answerdpo{} improves
ordinary short-answer behavior but weakens wrong-evidence rejection. Direct
verifier preferences, especially at 2k rows, improve explicit evidence
consistency without collapsing the short-answer gains. The remaining relation
failures are central to the result: a model can learn to reject many wrong
object and attribute labels while still accepting subject/object swaps.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We define \benchmark{}, a claim-evidence consistency benchmark that
    separates answer correctness from evidence correctness.
    \item We construct \dataset{}, a controlled 6k-row preference collection
    with object, attribute, relation, and same-answer wrong-evidence examples,
    plus fixed supported and wrong-evidence probes.
    \item We provide a deterministic protocol that reports short-answer
    transfer, support accuracy, wrong-evidence rejection, slice breakdowns,
    bootstrap intervals, and parser quality.
    \item We show in a five-group comparison that answer-only DPO can weaken
    wrong-evidence rejection, while direct verifier preferences improve the
    explicit probe and reveal relation role swaps as the main bottleneck.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Object hallucination and VLM evaluation.}
Object hallucination has been measured by comparing generated captions or
answers with visual annotations. CHAIR~\cite{chair} evaluates hallucinated
objects in captions, while POPE~\cite{pope} turns hallucination detection into
controlled yes/no polling. Broader public evaluations such as
MME~\cite{mme}, MMHal-Bench~\cite{llavarlhf}, AMBER~\cite{amber}, and
HallusionBench~\cite{hallusionbench} report hallucination, perception, and
reasoning failures across response-level or question-answer settings. These
benchmarks are useful for answer-level or caption-level behavior.
\benchmark{} asks a narrower question: given a candidate claim and a
candidate evidence item, does the model accept the evidence as support?

\paragraph{Preference tuning.}
DPO~\cite{dpo} and related preference objectives align models from paired
preferences. Recent multimodal alignment work extends this idea with
factually augmented RLHF~\cite{llavarlhf}, hallucination-aware DPO
preferences~\cite{hadpo}, fine-grained correctional feedback~\cite{rlhfv}, and
preference distillation~\cite{silkie}. In VLM settings, the easiest preference
pair often compares two final answers. Such supervision may improve the answer
distribution without forcing the model to inspect which visual fact supports
the answer. Our experiment keeps the optimizer and backbone fixed while
changing only whether the preference rows supervise answer choice, evidence
verification, or both.

\paragraph{Grounding and rationales.}
Grounded VLM training may use boxes, masks, region descriptions, or
human-written rationales, as in dense region annotation, referring-expression,
and explanation datasets~\cite{visualgenome,flickr30kentities,referitgame,vqax}.
These signals are stronger than the textual evidence labels used here, but
they are also more expensive. \benchmark{} takes a lighter route: it derives
object labels, attributes, and left/right relations from COCO and GQA
annotations. This makes the benchmark easy to regenerate and well suited for a
controlled diagnostic setting, at the cost of not measuring precise region
localization.

\paragraph{Structured evaluation.}
Many VLM failures are hidden when all outputs are reduced to a final answer.
\benchmark{} therefore requires a structured support decision and audits both
JSON-object parsing and fallback scoring. The purpose is not to reward verbose
explanations, but to expose whether a model accepts or rejects a specific
evidence item under a deterministic scoring rule.

\section{\benchmark{} Benchmark}

\subsection{Task Definition}

Each example contains an image $I$, a visual question $q$, a candidate claim
$c$, and candidate evidence $e$. The model receives the image and a prompt
similar to:
\begin{quote}
\small
\textit{Visual question: Is there a sink in the image? Candidate claim: sink
visible. Candidate evidence: label=potted plant. Decide whether the candidate
evidence supports the claim in the image.}
\end{quote}
The expected output is a JSON object with fields such as
\texttt{answer}, \texttt{claim}, \texttt{evidence\_label}, and
\texttt{support}. The support label is one of \texttt{supported},
\texttt{contradicted}, or \texttt{wrong\_evidence}. On wrong-evidence rows,
both \texttt{contradicted} and \texttt{wrong\_evidence} count as rejecting the
candidate evidence, because either decision means the evidence should not be
accepted as support for the claim. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} contrasts this
probe with ordinary answer-level evaluation.

\subsection{Data Construction}

\dataset{} is derived from COCO and GQA annotations. COCO contributes
object-existence claims with visible object labels and absent-object negatives.
GQA contributes color/material attributes and left/right spatial relations
from scene graphs. We also create same-answer wrong-evidence rows where the
answer text is intentionally not the discriminating signal: the candidate
evidence is a wrong object label, mismatched attribute, or reversed relation.
Examples include ``person visible'' with \texttt{label=airplane}, ``grass is
green'' with an unrelated object, and a left/right claim whose evidence swaps
subject and object. The answer remains plausible; the support decision should
change.

The supported-evidence probe contains 600 rows: 162 object, 276 attribute, and
162 relation examples. The wrong-evidence probe contains 400 rows: 126 object,
149 attribute, and 125 relation examples. The canonical data check reports no
blocking errors, no identical \answerdpo{} chosen/rejected rows, 1{,}000
same-answer wrong-evidence rows, and zero train/eval image overlap against
COCO held-out, GQA simple, Hard COCO, Base-error-mined, POPE, and AMBER evals.

We ran a 200-row annotation-consistency audit before training. All sampled
chosen answers match their supported claims (200/200), all applicable rejected
answers match contradicted claims (170/170), all chosen evidence entries match
the chosen claims (200/200), and all rejected errors match the intended
negative type (200/200). The remaining 30 rejected-answer checks are marked
not applicable because same-answer wrong-evidence rows intentionally keep the
answer plausible while changing only the evidence. The sample is stratified
across 60 object rows, 50 attribute rows, 60 relation rows, and 30
same-answer wrong-evidence rows.

\paragraph{Data card.}
The benchmark is intended for diagnostic evaluation of VLM grounding and
preference tuning. It uses annotation-derived labels from COCO and GQA, while
POPE and AMBER are used only as external evaluation sources. The repository
stores paths and metadata rather than redistributing images. Known limitations
include annotation incompleteness, weak textual evidence labels, missing boxes
for some GQA-derived rows, and narrow coverage of simple object, attribute,
and left/right relation claims.

\subsection{Verifier Preference Format}

The verifier preference rows use the same image and natural-language context
as the answer rows, but the chosen/rejected responses are structured evidence
judgments. For a supported object example, the chosen response marks
\texttt{support=supported} and repeats the candidate evidence label. For a
wrong-evidence example, the chosen response marks
\texttt{support=wrong\_evidence} or \texttt{support=contradicted}, while the
rejected response incorrectly accepts the mismatched evidence as supported.

\label{tab:format}

This format is intentionally simple. It does not require boxes, masks, or
human-written rationales. It only asks the model to decide whether the
candidate evidence item is a valid support for the claim. The lightweight
format keeps the data construction bounded for a diagnostic submission,
but it also explains a limitation of the results: success on \benchmark{} is
evidence-label consistency, not full visual grounding.

The separation between answer rows and verifier rows is important for
interpretation. Answer-DPO rows can improve the short-answer policy even if the
model never learns to reject an incorrect evidence label. Verifier rows can
teach a support decision, but by themselves they do not necessarily improve the
ordinary yes/no prompt. The mixed groups test whether these two supervision
signals can be combined without turning the study into a large data-scaling
exercise.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}

\subsection{Short-Answer Transfer}

Short-answer transfer uses four fixed yes/no evaluations. COCO object
existence and Hard COCO test object presence under normal and harder negative
sampling. GQA simple tests object, attribute, and relation questions from scene
graphs. Base-error-mined (BEM) recovery contains examples the base model gets
wrong; the base row is therefore 0 by construction, and the metric reports how
often tuning recovers those base errors. We report accuracy plus false-positive
rate (FPR) and false-negative rate (FNR) to expose yes/no bias.

\subsection{Claim-Evidence Metrics}

\benchmark{} reports supported-evidence accuracy on the 600-row supported
probe and wrong-evidence rejection on the 400-row wrong-evidence probe. The
wrong-evidence probe is further broken into object, attribute, and relation
slices. We also track whether the raw output contains a parseable JSON object,
the scored-output rate, and parse failure. The evidence probe uses ordinary
deterministic decoding rather than constrained decoding. JSON-object rate means
the scorer found a parseable JSON object in the raw output. Scored-output rate
also counts outputs recovered by deterministic fallback field parsing. Parse
failure counts rows with insufficient structure for scoring. For the primary
anchor comparison, we compute row-bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals with
10{,}000 resamples.

\subsection{Controls and Reporting Discipline}

We include several controls to keep the conclusion narrow. First, all training
and evaluation image identifiers are checked for overlap, so the probe is not
measuring repeated training images. Second, the supported and wrong-evidence
probes are fixed before comparing trained models. Third, the parser audit is
reported alongside the main probe metrics; a method should not receive credit
for a support decision if the scorer recovered only a malformed or ambiguous
output.
Image disjointness does not prove template-distribution independence: training
and probe rows still share annotation sources, prompt style, and evidence
templates. Paraphrased or human-written evidence probes remain future
external-validity checks.

The main table is also capped at five groups. This is a practical reporting
choice rather than an optimization claim. It keeps the paper focused on the
minimal scientific questions: whether answer-only DPO transfers to evidence
verification, whether verifier-only supervision is sufficient, and whether
mixed answer/verifier supervision improves the explicit probe. Additional
sensitivity runs are kept in the appendix and released artifacts.

Table~\ref{tab:short} shows that the answer-trained models preserve ordinary

probe behavior discussed next, but it is not a replacement for answer
preference tuning when the deployment prompt asks for a short answer.

Table~\ref{tab:probe} is the central diagnostic result. \answerdpo{} improves

answer-level gains cannot be treated as evidence-level gains.

Verifier supervision changes the picture. \evidencedpo{} raises
wrong-evidence rejection to 53.0\%, especially on object and attribute
mismatches, but it does not improve short-answer transfer. \dualonek{} combines
answer preferences with 1k verifier rows and improves supported-evidence
accuracy to 93.2\%, but wrong-evidence rejection remains only 48.5\%.
\dualtwok{} is the only main setting that clearly improves both sides of the
probe: supported-evidence accuracy reaches 95.2\% and wrong-evidence rejection
reaches 70.3\%.

\subsection{Slice and Error Analysis}

The slice columns in Table~\ref{tab:probe} show that \dualtwok{} does not
improve all evidence types equally. Object wrong-evidence rejection rises to
96.0\% and attribute wrong-evidence rejection rises to 85.9\%. Relation
wrong-evidence rejection reaches only 25.6\%, even though this is higher than
the base and answer-only rows. Row-bootstrap slice intervals for the three
anchor models are: object 96.0 [92.1, 99.2], attribute 85.9 [79.9, 91.3], and
relation 25.6 [18.4, 32.8] for \dualtwok{}. The relation interval remains low,
so the failure is not a small-sample artifact. A locked relation-stress probe
reaches 71.25\% overall rejection for \dualtwok{}, but separates easy
left/right reversals (100.0\%) from weak subject/object swaps (42.5\%).

Table~\ref{tab:external} checks whether the evidence-aware training causes an
obvious collapse on external yes/no hallucination tests. It does not:
\dualtwok{} stays close to \answerdpo{} on POPE and AMBER. The differences are
small, so we do not use them as the primary claim. They instead support a
negative control: the large \benchmark{} gain is not accompanied by a large
loss on ordinary external yes/no checks, but it is also not a broad external
benchmark breakthrough.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Answer and evidence behaviors separate.}
The most important empirical pattern is the disagreement between Tables
\ref{tab:short} and \ref{tab:probe}. \answerdpo{} improves ordinary answers
while lowering wrong-evidence rejection. \evidencedpo{} improves
wrong-evidence rejection while doing little for short-answer transfer.
\dualtwok{} combines both signals and gives the strongest evidence-probe
result. These behaviors would be hard to see from answer accuracy alone.

\paragraph{Verifier data scale matters, but the ablation has a confound.}
\dualonek{} improves supported-evidence accuracy but does not strongly reject
wrong evidence; \dualtwok{} is the first mixed recipe that improves both probe
sides. Its verifier rows are balanced as 1{,}000 supported and 1{,}000 wrong,
covering about 808 object, 607 attribute, and 585 relation examples. However,
CEPO-Dual-500, -1k, and -2k use 6.5k, 7k, and 8k total rows, so the 1k to 2k
jump shows that verifier volume and coverage matter, not a clean
mixture-ratio proof.

\paragraph{Relation evidence is the bottleneck.}
Object and attribute mismatches are often visible from a single label or
attribute. Relation mismatches require comparing two entities and preserving
their roles. The current textual evidence representation does not require the
model to identify regions, so it can accept a subject/object swap if the two
objects are both present. This is why we treat role-swap errors as a remaining
failure rather than a minor detail.

\paragraph{What the benchmark should and should not support.}
\benchmark{} supports a narrow scientific claim: evidence-verifier
preferences can improve a separately measured claim-evidence consistency
skill, and answer-only DPO can weaken that skill. It should not be used to
claim solved hallucination, robust localization, or general reasoning gains.
The external checks and relation failures are included specifically to keep
the conclusion calibrated.

\paragraph{Implications for data construction.}
The result argues for small but targeted evidence data rather than simply more
answer pairs. The 2k verifier rows are enough to expose a clear gain on object
and attribute wrong-evidence rejection, but they are not enough for reliable
relation verification. A follow-up data design should therefore spend new
annotation budget on harder relation contrasts and region-aware evidence
instead of increasing the same object-label template at larger scale.

\section{Limitations}

\benchmark{} is controlled and annotation-derived. It uses COCO and GQA
labels rather than newly collected human evidence labels. Textual evidence
labels are weaker than boxes, masks, or human rationales, and the rule-based
scorer cannot recognize every semantically valid free-form description. Some
GQA-derived rows lack boxes, so the current probe cannot verify region
localization. The experiment uses one 7B backbone, one LoRA-DPO recipe, and
one deterministic decoding setup. We add a three-seed check for the central
answer-versus-verifier comparison, but broader model families, paraphrased
templates, human-written evidence, and stronger verifier data would be needed
for a general alignment claim. Finally, the relation results show that
accepting or rejecting textual relation evidence is still far from a robust
spatial grounding test.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented \benchmark{}, a diagnostic benchmark for claim-evidence
consistency in VLM preference tuning. The benchmark separates ordinary
short-answer performance from the ability to verify whether a visual claim is
supported by the correct evidence. In a controlled five-group experiment,
\answerdpo{} improves short-answer behavior while weakening wrong-evidence
rejection, \evidencedpo{} improves verifier behavior without improving
short-answer transfer, and \dualtwok{} preserves answer gains while improving
explicit evidence consistency to 70.3\% wrong-evidence rejection. The remaining
relation failures are central to the conclusion: short-answer gains alone can
hide persistent wrong-evidence errors, so evidence consistency should be
measured directly.
